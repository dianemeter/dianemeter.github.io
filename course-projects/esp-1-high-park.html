Engineering Strategies & Practice I · Sep – Dec 2025

A safer way up the hill in High Park

On the walk from Keele Station to the High Park Nature Centre, one 9 m stretch of path climbs at 16°. That's far too steep for a wheelchair. This was my first client conceptual design project, which I got to work on during my first-term ESP course.

My role
Wrote our constraints, co-wrote our objectives, and wrote up one of our three final concepts
Team
Five first-year engineering students
Client
High Park Nature Centre
Outcome
The Third Wheel, a clip-on powered wheel for wheelchairs

Problem Statement

Lots of people struggle on that hill, including stroller users, people pulling wagons, and anyone with mobility challenges. But for wheelchair users, a 16° slope is hard and unsafe. So we focused our design on that user group.

We also scoped when the path would realistically be used. Leaving out winter and days with heavy rain or extreme heat, we found 208.6 days a year our design had to work for.

Quantifying Accessibility

"Make the park more accessible" is a great goal, but you can't compare designs against it. I co-wrote our objectives (what a good design should do, with a measurable target for each) and wrote our constraints (what any design must never do).

This was my first time learning engineering vocabulary and the rigorous steps of quantifying a problem and writing an engineering document. It's very different from the kind of writing you do anywhere else, so there were lots of learning curves.

I walked out with a much better idea of how to understand a problem: how to scope it, identify who it affects, and turn a client's needs into functions, objectives and constraints. That's vocabulary you hear in real-world engineering projects all the time.

Standards-based targets

Our targets came from sources like Ontario's accessibility legislation (AODA), the City of Toronto's Accessibility Design Guidelines and the CSA's non-slip surface standard, not from guesses.

Environmental constraints

High Park's Black Oak Savanna is a critically imperilled ecosystem. One of our constraints: no design can cross the fenced boundaries around the black oak trees. Others protect a service road and the off-leash dog areas.

Measurable metrics

For example, "resists moisture" became "absorbs no more than 0.5% water by weight after a standard 24-hour soak." I researched how that test is actually done.

Idea Generation and Selection

Using individual and group brainstorming, morphological charts and SCAMPER, our team came up with 142 ideas, from new paths and lifts to personal mobility devices. Then we filtered them:

  1. 142ideas generated
  2. 90after removing duplicates
  3. 63passed the feasibility check
  4. 12survived team multivoting
  5. 3best on low speed and low user effort
  6. 1chosen with a Pugh chart

Our three finalists were the Handcycle (arm pedals that attach to the wheelchair), the Elevator-Travelator (an elevator connected to a moving walkway), and the Third Wheel (a joystick-controlled powered wheel that clips onto the wheelchair). I wrote up the Elevator-Travelator and researched the extra dimensional requirements it would need to meet.

We recommended the Third Wheel. It met all nine objectives and all five constraints, with almost no effort needed from the user. Its trade-off: park staff have to attach it at the start of the route and remove it at the end.

What I took away

ESP I was my first time turning a client's request into something you can actually design for. Writing objectives and constraints taught me that the problem definition is half the design. Once "accessible" had numbers attached, choosing between 142 ideas became a lot less about opinion.
